Act 1

A language for taste

Professional tasters start with nine broad families. Is it fruity? Roasted? Nutty or chocolatey? Most cups can be placed in a ring this coarse within a sip or two.

Each family splits into narrower groups. “Fruity” becomes berry, citrus, dried fruit, or other fruit.

The outer ring holds the specific words you see on bags: 84 of them. Reading from the centre outwards is how tasters are trained to work: general first, specific last.

Follow one family. When a roaster writes “blackberry”, they are placing the coffee here: fruity, then berry, then one exact berry.

Your turn. Click any segment to zoom in, and click the centre to zoom back out.

The structure is adapted from the SCA & WCR Coffee Taster’s Flavor Wheel (2016) through BeanMap’s open version, which adds a few notes. The drawing is ours.

Act 2

From words to numbers

Words describe a coffee. A score judges it. Each dot is one lot of arabica cupped by certified Q graders for the Coffee Quality Institute: 207 lots from 22 places.

Above 80 points out of 100, a coffee counts as specialty. Only 3 lots fall below it. People mostly send their best coffee to be graded, so this is a collection of good coffees, not a sample of all coffee.

All 207 lots received the maximum sweetness score of 10.0. Under the cupping protocol used here, each cup that meets the sweetness criterion earns two points, up to ten.

Acidity scores vary across the same lots. Each dot sits at its recorded score, and dots stack vertically to keep every lot visible.

Each coloured dot is one cup-tested lot. The legend counts the lots in each processing group, and the horizontal axis shows their total cup scores. Notice how the three main groups overlap in these 207 samples.

Act 3

Where coffee grows

In 2024, Brazil and Vietnam grew nearly half of the world’s green coffee. Move the slider to see how harvests changed since 1961. Each circle represents one country or territory.

Click a country to zoom into its continent, then click again for a closer look. Hover over a country or dot for its annual tonnage. Data from FAOSTAT, cross-checked against Our World in Data.

Where do the beans go after harvest? Each arrow connects an exporter to an importer. Move the year slider or choose a trading area to explore its largest routes.

Select an arrow for its tonnage. The lines connect trading partners and show direction. Source: CEPII BACI, based on UN Comtrade.

Back to the cup: do coffees grown higher up score better? Among the 202 CQI lots with recorded altitude, higher elevation was weakly associated with higher scores.

Follow the highest point, a Castillo lot from Colombia that scored 89.33.

Act 4

From cherry to bean

Wild Arabica grows in the forests of southwestern Ethiopia and on the Boma Plateau in South Sudan. The earliest documented cultivation dates to around the 15th century in Yemen.
